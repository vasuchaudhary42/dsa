How two pointers really works

Thirteen strategies sound like thirteen things to memorise. They are not. Every one of them is the same idea, a search that throws away candidates it can prove useless, applied to one of three shapes with one of four kinds of proof.

The search-space view

Every problem here has a set of candidate answers: all pairs (i, j), all positions, all ways to split. Brute force looks at every candidate. Two pointers keeps a small frontier, usually the two indices, and at each step proves that some candidates can never be the answer, then discards them without looking. Pair sum discards a whole row or column of the n × n pair grid per step. That is why n² candidates are settled in n steps.

Three shapes

Converging

▲ l▲ r
Every candidate outside [l, r] is already settled.

Start at both ends and move inward. Strategies 1–6.

Same direction

▲ w▲ r
Everything behind the slower pointer is final.

Both move forward, at different speeds or with a gap. Strategies 7–9 and 13.

One per sequence

▲ i
▲ j
Everything before i and j is already resolved.

One pointer per input, advancing the side that cannot match later. Strategy 10. Strategies 11 and 12 are variants that move outward or from the right.

Four kinds of proof

Every pointer move needs a one-sentence reason. There are only four kinds, and naming the kind tells you what to say in an interview.

ProofThe sentence you sayUsed by
Elimination“This candidate is too big even with the smallest partner left, so nothing that uses it can work.”1 Pair sum, 2 k-Sum, 3 Counting, 9 Gap, 10 Two sequences
Dominance or exchange“Any answer that keeps this side is no better than one I have already measured,” or “swapping my choice into any optimal answer keeps it optimal.”4 Container, 6 Greedy pairing
Settled state“The answer at this index depends only on what I have already seen on its side.”5 Trapping water
Invariant“After every step, this region is exactly right, and the unknown region got smaller.”7 Read/write, 8 Partition, 11 Expand, 12 Scan from right, 13 Fast and slow

Why it is O(n): the shrinking-distance argument

For converging pointers, track d = r − l. Every iteration moves at least one pointer inward, so d drops by at least 1. It starts at n − 1 and the loop stops at 0, so there are at most n − 1 iterations. Inner skip loops, such as 3Sum's duplicate skipping, also shrink d, so they are paid for by the same budget. For same-direction pointers, track d = 2n − i − j. Each pointer only moves forward, so there are at most 2n moves in total. This is the argument to give when an interviewer asks, “Isn't that nested while loop O(n²)?”

DecisionRuleExample
Loop conditionl < r when the answer needs two different elements. l <= r when every element must be handled once, including a lone middle one.Pair sum uses l < r. Boats, squares of a sorted array and the Dutch flag (mid <= high) need the equals.
Can I sort?Sort freely when the output is values, counts or a yes/no answer. If you must return original indices, sort an array of indices, or use a hash map.3Sum returns values, so sort. Two Sum returns indices, so use a hash map.
What about duplicates?Decide which of three things the problem counts: pairs of indices (never skip), unique value pairs (skip after each hit), or unique output tuples (skip anchors and inner values).Counting pairs below a target counts indices.
